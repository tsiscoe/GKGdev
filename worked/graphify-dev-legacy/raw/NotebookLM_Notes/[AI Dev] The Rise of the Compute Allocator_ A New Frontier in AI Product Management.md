# \[AI Dev\] The Rise of the Compute Allocator: A New Frontier in AI Product Management

## 1. Introduction: Beyond the Traditional PRD

For decades, the path to building software began with the Product Requirement Document (PRD)—a static, text-heavy monument to intent. But as AI agents like Claude transition from simple chat interfaces to long-running systems capable of eight-hour autonomous streaks, the "document writer" is becoming an artifact of the past.

In this new landscape, we aren't just writing specs; we are managing high-stakes budgets of machine intelligence. As Thariq Shihipar of the Claude Code team puts it, "Product Management isn't dead—it has evolved." We are witnessing the birth of the \*\*Compute Allocator\*\*.

> "All of us are becoming these compute allocators now... you have to decide what is worthwhile spending the compute on. You're writing documents to decide whether or not something else should do work in the shape of that work."

The "so what" for the modern builder is clear: understanding this shift is the only way to remain relevant. Your job is no longer to micromanage the \*how\*, but to define the \*\*shape of the work\*\* and decide whether a specific problem warrants a $500 investment in compute.

\*\*The Transition:\*\* We are moving away from static checklists and toward "Compute Budgets," where the plan is no longer just a reference—it is the primary tool for financial and strategic oversight.

---

## 2. Defining the Compute Allocator

To be a Compute Allocator is to recognize that machine time is a finite, valuable resource. When you grant an agent like Claude Code permission to run for eight hours to solve a complex architectural problem, you are making a $500 financial decision.

The modern builder doesn't focus on implementation details; they focus on the \*\*Bookends\*\*: the \*\*Type Interfaces\*\* (the data model) and the \*\*Verification Rubrics\*\* (the success criteria).

### The 3 Core Responsibilities of a Compute Allocator

1.  \*\*Strategic Resource Allocation:\*\* Determining which features or bug fixes are worth a $500 "compute run." This is the "PRD Diet" phase—cutting the fat and focusing on high-compute-value problems.
2.  \*\*Unknown Unknown Discovery:\*\* Using the agent as a collaborative partner to pull hidden complexities out of your own head. The spec process becomes an interview where the AI helps you find what you didn't know you missed.
3.  \*\*Outcome Management:\*\* Shifting from "Make no mistakes" to "Managed Outcomes." This involves defining technical success criteria and using agents to record videos of their work as proof of verification.

### The PM Paradigm Shift

| Feature | Old Way (Static Lists) | New Way (Compute Allocation) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| \*\*Primary Tool\*\* | Markdown / Static PRDs | Living Artifacts & Verification Rubrics |
| \*\*Human Role\*\* | Writing instructions | Managing compute spend & "Shape of Work" |
| \*\*Technical Focus\*\* | Implementation details | Type Interfaces (The "Bookends") |
| \*\*Output\*\* | Manual code review | "In-loop" interactive engagement |

This shift requires a fundamental change in the medium we use to communicate our vision to the machine.

## 3. Why HTML is the New Markdown

For years, Markdown was the gold standard for AI interaction. However, as agents have become capable of generating thousands of lines of code over hour-long runs, Markdown has hit a "human wall." A thousand-line text file is an "eye-crossing" experience that causes humans to stop reading and lose the loop.

As seen in the recent "Code with Claude" developer conference, \*\*HTML has surpassed Markdown\*\* as the preferred medium for high-intelligence agents for three strategic reasons:

1.  \*\*Superior Scrollability & Structure:\*\* HTML allows for navigable layouts that humans actually engage with. You can scroll through eight visual ideas in HTML; you stop reading after three in Markdown.
2.  \*\*Technical Richness:\*\* Markdown relies on "ASCII art" mockups. HTML allows Claude to render actual interactive diagrams, mood boards, and "Vibe-coded" feature flag dashboards that you can actually see and feel.
3.  \*\*The "In-Sync" Factor:\*\* High-intelligence models like Claude 3.5 are native in HTML. Using it allows for a richer communication loop where the human stays engaged with the plan rather than abdicating responsibility to a text wall.

## 4. The Power of "Micro-Software" and Throwaway UIs

The most revolutionary tool in the Compute Allocator’s kit is the creation of \*\*"sub-micro software"\*\*—temporary, custom interfaces designed to solve a single sub-problem.

Instead of arguing with an agent in a terminal about complex data rules, you ask the agent to build a \*\*Throwaway UI\*\*. A classic example is the \*\*"CSV to Interactive Dashboard"\*\* workflow: if you have a complex data set, you don't edit a text table; you prompt the agent to build a custom dashboard with knobs and sliders that let you "gamify" the decision-making process.

### Steps to Creating a Throwaway UI:
- \[ \] \*\*Identify the Friction:\*\* Find a decision rule or data set that is too dense for text conversation.
- \[ \] \*\*Prompt the Interface:\*\* Instruct the agent: "Create a custom HTML interface that helps me visualize and edit these rules. Design the ideal UI for this specific problem."
- \[ \] \*\*Engage & Refine:\*\* Use the sliders and toggles in the "consumery" UI to find the right settings.
- \[ \] \*\*Sync the Result:\*\* Export the refined data or Markdown back into the main project.

These aren't permanent features; they are "just-in-time documentation" tools that allow you to stay perfectly in sync with the agent’s logic.

## 5. A New Way of Working: The "In-Loop" Relationship

The ultimate goal of a Compute Allocator is to stay "in the loop" without micromanaging. This requires a \*\*"Trust but Verify"\*\* philosophy. You focus on the "Bookends"—the data model/types and the verification criteria—while letting the agent handle the "gravy" in the middle.

Instead of over-constraining the model with 100-line prompts that stifle creativity, give the model permission to explore. Verification can even be automated; for example, having an agent record a video of its own execution as a "managed outcome" for your review.

> \*\*Pro-Tip: Lead with Trust\*\*
> Don't prompt with "Make no mistakes." Instead, lean into the "Anthropic-culture" approach: \*\*"I trust your judgment—provide whatever is needed for maximum context."\*\* Leaving the path open for the model to decide \*how\* to present information (e.g., creating a mood board or a living design system) often results in more evocative and technically sound insights.

## 6. Conclusion: Your Roadmap as a Compute Allocator

Documentation is not dead; it has become a \*\*Living Design System\*\*. In this new era, your most valuable artifact isn't a static document, but a repository of components and interactive specs that evolve with the code. Whether you are "vibe coding" a new dashboard or managing a "PRD Diet," your success depends on your ability to allocate compute to the right "shape of work."

### Checklist for Success
\*   \*\*Define the Bookends:\*\* Focus your energy on the \*\*Type Interfaces\*\* (data model) and the \*\*Verification Rubric\*\*. Let the agent handle the implementation "gravy."
\*   \*\*Adopt HTML Artifacts:\*\* Move your planning from \`.md\` to \`.html\`. Use visual guides, interactive mood boards, and component variations to stay engaged.
\*   \*\*Build Micro-Apps:\*\* If a sub-problem is hard to wrap your head around, spend the compute to build a throwaway UI to visualize the solution.
\*   \*\*Manage by Outcomes:\*\* Use "Managed Agents" to verify work. Ask for a video recording or a specific success rubric instead of just checking code lines.
\*   \*\*Stay "In-Sync":\*\* Remember, you're a compute allocator, babe. Your value is in the decisions you make about \*what\* is worth the machine's time.

By mastering these interactive artifacts, you move from being an AI user to a master \*\*Compute Allocator\*\*, building complex, beautiful software at the speed of thought.
